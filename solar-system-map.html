Distance

The solar system spans a distance so vast that a straight-line map would make the inner planets invisible. Distances from the Sun use a logarithmic scale, so every ring further out represents a much bigger real jump than the one before it. The scale bar (bottom left) shows the true local scale near the center of your current view — it stretches out again as you look further from the Sun, since that's the nature of a compressed map.

Size

No single map can show true sizes and true distances together — Earth would be a pixel a football field away from a beach-ball Sun. Marker sizes are scaled to each other by real diameter, on their own separate scale from distance.

Tilt & rotation

Drag the small dial (bottom left) to rotate and tilt your view in 3D — or, with a touchscreen, use two fingers: pinch to zoom, twist to spin around the Sun, and drag two fingers up/down to tilt. One finger always pans. Planets, dwarf planets, comets and named asteroids are tilted by their real orbital inclination — that's why Pluto and especially Eris and comet Halley swing so far off the flat plane when you tilt the camera. Moon and star tilts are simplified for visual variety, not precise.

Focus

Selecting a body makes it the pivot for rotating and the anchor for zooming — the camera orbits around whatever you last selected, keeping it centered no matter how you spin the view. With nothing selected, that's the Sun.

Position

Except for inclination, angles are illustrative — spread out for readability, not where each body actually sits on a given date. For the nearby-star layer, distances from the Sun are real; directions are arbitrary.

Zooming out

As you zoom out, detail thins automatically: moons drop away first, then planets, dwarf planets, comets, asteroids and the dust belts, leaving the Sun and — once you're far enough out to reach real interstellar distances — the Oort Cloud and nearby stars.
